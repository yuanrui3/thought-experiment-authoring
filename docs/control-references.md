## Expected sizes: confirmed by the project owner

I had set "A higher, small" for the dressing and brakes controls from memory. Having read the papers, I recommended keeping both, for the reasons below. The project owner confirmed "small" for both on 15 November 2026.

- **Dressing.** Between 30% and 65% of people judged the commission worse, and those who did differed by 27.6 points of 100 on average. Spread over everyone, that is a modest average gap, which fits "small" (0.5 to 1.5 points on a 7-point scale). The mapping between a 100-point and a 7-point scale is my own rough arithmetic.
- **Brakes.** Blame rose by d = 0.39 and 0.33 in the two stories tested. That is a small-to-medium effect, which fits "small".

## Checked

### Powder in the coffee

Source read in full: Young, Cushman, Hauser & Saxe (2007), "The neural basis of the interaction between theory of mind and moral judgment", PNAS 104(20): 8235–8240, on [PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1895935/).

- **Design.** A brain-imaging study. "Ten naive right-handed subjects (Harvard College undergraduates…)". Belief (harmful or harmless) was crossed with outcome (harm or none); "Subjects saw two variations of each scenario, for a total of 24 stories."
- **Measure.** "Subjects evaluated the moral status of protagonists' actions using four buttons on a scale from 1 for completely forbidden to 4 for completely permissible."
- **Result.** With a harmless belief, a harmful outcome averaged 3.2 and a harmless one 3.9. With a harmful belief the averages were 1.1 and 1.2. So harm done knowingly scored 1.1 and the same harm done unknowingly scored 3.2.
- **What I had wrong.** I wrote "less blameworthy" and "large effect" with no numbers. The paper measures permissibility, not blame, in ten people who each saw several conditions.
- **Still open.** The scenario's exact wording is in the paper's supplement, which would not open. The main text confirms its content: "the identification of the white powder by the coffee as poison rather than sugar".
- **This panel.** Blame of 5.85 against 1.55 on a 7-point scale. Same direction; a different measure, so sizes are not comparable.

### The house dressing

Source read (Experiments 1 and 2): Spranca, Minsk & Baron (1991), "Omission and commission in judgment and choice", Journal of Experimental Social Psychology 27: 76–105, from a copy of [Baron's page](https://www.sas.upenn.edu/~baron/papers.htm/oc.html) supplied by the project owner.

- **The case.** John West, the best player at his club, meets Ivan Lendl in the final. He "remembers reading that Ivan is allergic to Cayenne pepper" and that the house dressing contains it, and thinks: "If Ivan eats the house dressing, he will probably get a stomach ache that will keep him up much of the night. Then I'll have a chance to win."
- **Experiment 1.** 57 students rated "John's morality in this situation for each of the six endings on a scale from 0 (not immoral at all) to -100". In the endings where the ruse works, John recommends the dressing before Ivan chooses; or Ivan orders it himself and "John says nothing"; or "Ivan orders Italian dressing. John then recommends that Ivan try the house dressing."
- **Result of Experiment 1.** "In case 1, 37 out of 57 subjects (65%) rated each omission as less bad than either corresponding commission, showing an omission bias, and only one subject (for idiosyncratic reasons) rated an omission as worse than a commission." Among those who drew a distinction, the gap "ranged from 1 point (five subjects) to 70 points on the 100 point scale (mean, 27.6; s.d., 21.2; median 25)."
- **Experiment 2.** Two endings: "Before Ivan makes a choice, John recommends that Ivan try the house dressing", and "John is about to recommend the house dressing, but before John can say anything, Ivan orders the house dressing himself. John says nothing." Of 36 participants, 33 rated John's intention as equivalent; of those, "10 rated his commission as more immoral than the omission, and none rated the omission as more immoral".
- **Design choice that matters here.** The endings were shown side by side on purpose: "if we had used a between-subjects design, subjects in the commission condition would very likely infer stronger intention than those in the omission condition." My control shows each reader one version, which is the design the authors avoided.
- **What I had wrong.** My first text said "a sizeable minority rate the commission as worse". That describes Experiment 2 only. The 65% relayed by a web search is Experiment 1. Both are real and both are now given.
- **How my paraphrase maps.** Version A follows Experiment 1's third ending; version B follows Experiment 2's omission ending. Version B leaves out a sentence Experiment 1 includes, that John "realizes that if he had warned Ivan… Ivan would have ordered Italian dressing".
- **This panel.** Wrongness of 5.75 against 4.60, a gap of 1.15 points.
- **Dropped.** The meta-analysis I had cited (Yeung, Yay & Feldman 2022) was never opened; the figure I held for it came from a search summary of its abstract.

### The American car

Sources read in full: Weinberg, Nichols & Stich (2001), "Normativity and Epistemic Intuitions", Philosophical Topics 29(1&2): 429–460 ([author-hosted copy](https://www.stephenstich.com/_files/ugd/0ad9d7_cf31abf2f7c048f38492daecec85340b.pdf)); Machery, Stich, Rose, Chatterjee, Karasawa, Struchiner, Sirker, Usui & Hashimoto, "Gettier Across Cultures", Noûs, online 2015, doi 10.1111/nous.12110 ([author-hosted copy](https://www.stephenstich.com/_files/ugd/0ad9d7_3940f0aae17342468149cfe5c61b4e95.pdf)).

- **The case.** The original reads: "Bob has a friend, Jill, who has driven a Buick for many years. Bob therefore thinks that Jill drives an American car. He is not aware, however, that her Buick has recently been stolen, and he is also not aware that Jill has replaced it with a Pontiac, which is a different kind of American car. Does Bob really know that Jill drives an American car, or does he only believe it?" My paraphrase keeps this structure.
- **Result.** Forced choice. Western participants: 17 "really knows", 49 "only believes" (74% deny knowledge). East Asian: 13 and 10. Indian subcontinent: 14 and 9. The paper's point was the difference: "among EAs this pattern is actually reversed! A majority of EAs say that Bob really knows."
- **Machery et al.** 245 participants in the final sample, in the USA, Brazil, India and Japan. They used two other Gettier cases (a hospital case and a trip case), not the car case, plus an unrelated clear-knowledge story and a false-belief story, all shown to every participant in a fixed order. "participants were much less likely to ascribe knowledge in either Gettier case than in the clear knowledge case", with 64.9% to 94.6% choosing "feels like she knows … but she doesn't actually know", and "no difference in knowledge ascription between the USA, Brazil, India, and Japan" on that question.
- **What I had wrong.** I wrote that people attribute knowledge in "matched" clear-knowledge cases and that the contrast "holds across the cultures tested". The original car study has no control version and found the opposite across cultures. The later study supports a cross-cultural Gettier intuition, but with different cases and an unmatched control.
- **Consequence.** The clear-knowledge version of this control is my own writing. No human data exist for the contrast the panel was tested on. Only the Gettier version has a human number.
- **This panel.** Agreement that he knows: 6.15 in the clear version, 3.60 in the Gettier version, on a 7-point scale.

## Partly checked

### The unchecked brakes

Source read: Kneer & Machery (2019), "No luck for moral luck", Cognition 182, from a copy of the article supplied by the project owner ([PhilArchive record](https://philarchive.org/rec/KNENLF); the page range 331–348 is from that record, since the copy is an in-press proof). Not opened: Nagel (1979), "Moral Luck", in Mortal Questions ([publisher's page](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/books/mortal-questions/moral-luck/A3EEA631B0CA6F1A322A56E85FB77DB4)).

- **The stories tested.** Not a driver. "Anna is at home, giving her 2-year-old son a bath… Anna leaves the room for 5 min. When Anna returns, her son is in the tub, dead, face down in the water." In the other version the boy "is still standing near the tub". A second story, in Study 4, involves lead paint. The driver appears only as the paper's opening illustration.
- **Measures.** Four questions on 7-point scales, among them "To what extent was Anna [Beth] blameworthy for leaving her son alone in the above scenario?"
- **One outcome per person (Study 1a, 196 people).** Effect of the bad outcome: wrongness d = .44, blame d = .39, permissibility d = .26 (p = .07), punishment d = .79. The authors' summary: "a medium to large effect of outcome on punishment judgments, but only a small to medium effect of outcome on wrongness and blame judgments."
- **Second story (Study 4a).** Punishment d = 1.07; wrongness d = .36; blame d = .33; no reliable effect on permissibility.
- **Both outcomes side by side (Study 1b, 95 people).** "The vast majority of participants did not share the Difference Intuition for wrongness (89%), blame (87%), and permissibility (96%), though the proportion was substantially smaller for punishment (64%)."
- **What I had wrong.** I wrote that wrongness "moves little" between participants. It moves about as much as blame. A web search had also relayed that outcome affects "all four" judgments; the abstract says so, but in both between-participant studies the effect on permissibility was not statistically reliable.
- **Consequence.** The human data come from other negligence stories. No one has measured the brake-inspection case.
- **Still open.** Nagel's own wording of the driver case. I know it only from secondary descriptions.
- **This panel.** Blame of 4.75 against 4.15, a gap of 0.60 points. Standardised against the spread of all judges' ratings, that is 0.61, larger than the human 0.33 to 0.39 because the panel's ratings vary less.

### The pens on the desk

Source read in full: Hitchcock & Knobe (2009), "Cause and Norm", Journal of Philosophy 106(11): 587–612, in the [PhilSci Archive preprint](https://philsci-archive.pitt.edu/8352/). It reproduces the story and reports the results of Knobe & Fraser (2008), whose chapter is not openly available.

- **The case.** "The administrative assistants are allowed to take pens, but faculty members are supposed to buy their own… one of the administrative assistants encounters Professor Smith walking past the receptionist's desk. Both take pens… There are no pens left on her desk."
- **Result.** "Subjects were presented with one of the two statements, and were asked to rate the extent to which they agreed or disagreed with on a 7 point scale… 'Professor Smith caused the problem' gave an average rating of 5.2, while… 'The administrative assistant caused the problem' gave an average rating of 2.8. The results were statistically significant (p < .001)."
- **Second story in the same paper.** A doctor who breaks hospital policy and a pharmacist who does not both sign off on a drug: 3.9 against 2.5 on the same scale, 3,422 participants.
- **What I had wrong.** I cited Hitchcock & Knobe and Icard, Kominsky & Knobe (2017) for "manipulating norm status across conditions". Hitchcock & Knobe compare two agents inside one story; they do not vary one agent's permission between conditions. Icard et al. could not be opened. The claim and the citation are removed.
- **Consequence.** My control varies the professor's permission between versions and asks only about him. No human data exist for the version in which he may take pens, so the human result supports the expected direction by extension only.
- **This panel.** 4.65 in the rule-breaking version, against the human 5.2 on the same scale and near-identical wording; 2.95 when he is allowed.
