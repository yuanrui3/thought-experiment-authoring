# Prior-art check: Thought-experiment authoring

Date: 14 November 2026. Window searched: roughly November 2023 to November 2026, plus older work where it was among the closest.

## Bottom line

**Verdict: clear to proceed.** Nothing found does what this benchmark does. Every individual ingredient exists somewhere, so the project is an assembly of known parts aimed at a gap, and its claims have to be worded accordingly. The reasoning and the adjustments I will make are in the last two sections.

## What "the same thing" means here

The benchmark has four defining features. A match would need most of them together:

1. The model under test is the **author**: given one named factor, it writes a free-text minimal pair of vignettes.
2. The author **predicts** the direction and size of the difference in readers' judgments.
3. Scoring is **behavioral**: a blind panel, each member seeing one version, and the score is whether ratings split as predicted.
4. The panel itself is tested with **classic-pair and null-pair controls**, and authored pairs are checked for **confounds and originality**.

## The five closest matches

### 1. OffTheRails: closest in content

Fränken, Gandhi, Qiu, Khawaja, Goodman & Gerstenberg (2024), "Procedural Dilemma Generation for Evaluating Moral Reasoning in Humans and Language Models", CogSci 2024. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.10975), [repository](https://github.com/cicl-stanford/moral-evals). **Read in full** (arXiv HTML); the repository's data was read during the sweeps.

- **Measures:** the moral judgments of people and of two models (GPT-4, Claude 2) on machine-written dilemmas. The writing model is a tool, not the thing evaluated.
- **How items are made:** researchers fix a causal template with three factors (means vs side effect, evitable vs inevitable, action vs omission); GPT-4 fills the slots; sentences are stitched into 8 conditions per scenario.
- **Ground truth:** two preregistered Prolific studies. In the main one, 100 participants each rated 16 items for permissibility and intention, giving 20 ratings per item. The design is within-participant.
- **Data:** 50 scenarios and 400 items; 80 items have human ratings. Public, MIT licence. The released file shows 115 worker IDs against the paper's 100; I did not resolve the difference.
- **Result worth knowing:** two factors moved ratings as the researchers predicted; action vs omission showed "no credible effect", and the authors call their effects "rather weak". They also report that GPT-4 "failed to reliably distinguish between means and side effects during generation".
- **Differs from this project:** the author is not scored, ranked or compared across models; the researchers, not the author, make the predictions; items come from a fixed template, not free writing for a named factor; no null pairs, no classic-case controls, no originality requirement; moral factors only.

### 2. Automated Social Science: closest in loop shape

Manning, Zhu & Horton (2024), "Automated Social Science: Language Models as Scientist and Subjects", arXiv / NBER working paper. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.11794). **Read in part** (abstract, introduction, the prediction section and the future-work section of the arXiv HTML).

- **Measures:** whether a system can propose hypotheses, design experiments, run them on language-model agents and analyse the results without a person.
- **Ground truth:** the simulated experiments' own estimates. No human participants; the authors note "the fundamental jump from simulations to human subjects".
- **Data:** four scenarios (bargaining, bail hearing, job interview, auction), three proposed causes each. GPT-4 for everything.
- **Result worth knowing:** asked to predict its own experiments' results, the model got the sign right in 10 of 12 cases, but its predicted magnitudes were on average 13.2 times too large.
- **Differs from this project:** a single-model system demonstration, not a benchmark of designers; manipulations are attributes of simulated agents in a structural causal model, not matched text pairs; no confound, originality or null controls; social science, not philosophy.

### 3. The Counterexample Game: closest in the skill measured

Drucker & Mahowald (2026), "The Counterexample Game: Iterated Conceptual Analysis and Repair in Language Models", CogSci 2026. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.03936). **Read in full** (arXiv HTML).

- **Measures:** whether models can write valid counterexample cases to proposed definitions and repair the definitions. This is the only work found where authoring philosophical cases is itself the measured skill.
- **Ground truth:** validity verdicts from five trained raters (the two authors and three philosophy graduate students) on 60 cases, and from a model judge (Claude Opus 4.5) on about 3,000.
- **Data:** 20 everyday concepts; 6,000 mixed-model cycles and 1,200 single-model cycles. No data release is mentioned in the paper.
- **Result worth knowing:** the model judge accepted roughly twice as many counterexamples as the human raters, and human raters agreed with each other only moderately.
- **Differs from this project:** single cases, not matched pairs; scored by expert and model-judge opinion, not by readers' behavior; no prediction; no check for memorised or famous cases; no null controls.

### 4. HumanStudy-Bench: closest on the panel and scoring half

Liu et al. (2026), "HumanStudy-Bench: Towards AI Agent Design for Participant Simulation". [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.00685), [repository](https://github.com/AISmithLab/HumanStudy-Bench). **Read in part** (introduction, task and metric sections of the arXiv HTML); the repository was read during the sweeps.

- **Measures:** how well language-model agents stand in for human participants. Published experiments are replayed on the agents and analysed with each study's original statistics.
- **Ground truth:** the published human results of 12 classic studies, one of which is Knobe's 2003 side-effect study.
- **Data:** 12 studies, 6,000+ trials, 10 models and four agent designs; open platform with a leaderboard.
- **Scoring:** two separate scores, one for reaching the same conclusion about whether an effect exists and one for matching its size. Size agreement is "uniformly low" across all agents. It also includes paraphrase and recognition controls for memorised studies.
- **Differs from this project:** nothing is authored; all stimuli are published human-written studies. It already does, more carefully, what this project's classic controls do.

### 5. PhilosophyBench: closest in positioning

Stanford University study, announced September 2026 according to the [Daily Nous report](https://dailynous.com/2026/09/25/new-study-on-ais-philosophical-skills/) (the site itself gives no date). [Site](https://philosophybench.org/), [FAQ](https://philosophybench.org/faq). **Read in full** (home page and FAQ, the only public content pages; the rest is sign-up and sign-in).

- **Measures:** whether AI systems can write original philosophy essays in response to examination questions set by philosophers, and how much AI access improves human writers.
- **Ground truth:** blind grading and structured feedback by people with philosophical training. An experimental automatic grader is planned.
- **Data:** private by design; no results or paper yet.
- **Differs from this project:** essays, not thought experiments; no task on constructing cases or vignettes appears anywhere on the site; scored by expert opinion; nothing behavioral.

### Also close, but only the abstract could be read

Brondetta & Musslick (2026), "Automated Prototyping of Behavioral Experiments with Large Language Models", a workshop paper at ICML 2026. [Listing](https://icml.cc/virtual/2026/73542). A model iteratively rewrites an experiment's cover story to steer the behavior of language-model participants on one cognitive task. That is authored experimental text scored by participant behavior, which is close in spirit. It is optimisation on one task, with no stated prediction, matched pair or author comparison, as far as the abstract shows. The full text is on OpenReview, which refused automated access; I did not try to get round that.

## Other related work

Checked against arXiv for title and authors; abstracts read where marked, otherwise known from the sweeps' repository reads and search summaries.

- **Language-model panels misfire on nulls.** Cui, Li & Zhou, a replication of 156 experiments with three models ([abstract read](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.00128)): effect sizes two to three times the human ones, and significant results in 68–83% of cases where the human original was null. Gui & Toubia ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.15524)) argue that blinded model subjects treat a manipulated text as a cue about unstated context.
- **The generator scored by behavior, in NLP.** CEval ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17475), [repository](https://github.com/aix-group/CEval-Counterfactual-Generation-Benchmark)) ranks writers of minimal edits by whether a classifier's label flips and how small the edit is. Categorical labels and a classifier, not graded human judgments or predicted effects.
- **Vignettes with human judgments.** MoCa ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.19677), [repository](https://github.com/cicl-stanford/moca)): 206 published causal and moral stories with 25 yes/no human votes each and factor annotations. Almeida et al. ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.01264)): replications of experimental-philosophy studies with models.
- **Predicting experimental effects.** Hewitt, Ashokkumar, Ghezae & Willer ([project page](https://ai4pb.stanford.edu/projects/predicting-results-of-social-science-experiments-using-large-language-models)): model predictions correlate strongly with real effects in survey experiments but overestimate their size. Not read at source.
- **Model-written test items.** BigToM ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15448)), DailyDilemmas ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02683)), delta-Rules-of-Thumb ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.15431)), and a creative item generator for psychometrics ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.00202)). The model makes the data; it is not the examinee.
- **Agents that design and run experiments.** auto-psych ([abstract read](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.26460)) and Wehr et al. ([abstract read](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.13421)) run studies on real participants; SCOPE ([abstract read](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03501)) grades experiment plans against published machine-learning papers; BoxingGym ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.01540)) scores experimenters against simulators. None scores an author of vignettes.
- **Other philosophy evaluations of generation.** Choi et al. on model-written moral alternatives ([abstract read](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.31213)); the AI Philosophy Competition ([announcement](https://dailynous.com/2026/09/01/testing-ais-philosophical-writing-with-a-contest/)); the Conceptual Reasoning Index from Redwood Research and Anthropic ([post](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/conceptual-reasoning-index/)), which scores argument quality, belief consistency and decision-theory questions. The last two are known from search summaries only.
- **Names already taken:** PhilosophyBench (Stanford), [philosophy-bench](https://github.com/benedictbrady/philosophy-bench) (tool-use ethics scenarios), PhilBench. "Thought-experiment authoring" does not collide with any of them.

## What the prior art changes

None of this changes the angle or the work already saved. I will make these adjustments when the build resumes:

1. **Narrower claim.** The project must not be described as the first benchmark of philosophical generation; that would be false. The defensible description is: scoring authored thought experiments by how readers respond, not by a grader's opinion.
2. **Direction first, size second.** Two independent sources found that models predict the sign of an effect well and its size badly, and model panels inflate sizes. Direction and size will be reported as separate components, as HumanStudy-Bench does, and size results on a Claude-only panel will be labelled as not transferable to people.
3. **Stronger warning on the panel, with numbers.** The 68–83% false-positive figure on human-null studies goes into the methods note next to this pilot's own null-pair rate.
4. **Test the auditors, not only the panel.** The confound and originality components rest on model auditors, and the Counterexample Game shows a model judge accepting twice what experts accept. I will add a few pairs with a deliberately planted confound, shown to auditors only, to measure whether they catch it; the paraphrased classics already test whether they spot re-skins.
5. **Reuse, in next steps.** Human-rated material already exists for anchoring a later human round: OffTheRails' 80 rated items, MoCa's near-minimal pairs, and the replication archives for classic effects. OffTheRails also shows that machine-written manipulations produce small effects in people, so the size bins will need re-calibrating on human data.

## Verdict

**Clear to proceed.** The specific combination this benchmark tests was not found in about 200 queries across four fields' vocabularies, and the five closest sources, read directly, each cover one part of it and none covers the whole. The build resumes where it paused, with the five adjustments above, and the methods note will cite OffTheRails, Automated Social Science, the Counterexample Game, HumanStudy-Bench, PhilosophyBench and Cui, Li & Zhou.
